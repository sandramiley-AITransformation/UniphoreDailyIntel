Summary for Umesh

The AI-CEO debate just split three ways — pick the lane that doesn't require taking a side

This week's clearest shift is that the AI-risk conversation among these five CEOs has hardened from a vague sense of "governance pressure is rising" into an explicit, public three-way split. Huang is now naming Altman and Amodei directly and calling their doom framing "irresponsible," putting the odds of AI "ending the world" at a flat "0%." Amodei hasn't engaged that challenge publicly — he's still absorbing a sharpened, concrete ask from NYC's comptroller (pause the IPO, run safety audits, respond in writing) even as Anthropic reportedly keeps its Nasdaq listing on track. And Ghodsi has quietly carved out a third position that sidesteps the whole fight: AI risk is real, but it's mundane and operational — cybersecurity, not science fiction — and deserves investment, not philosophy. Karp, meanwhile, keeps pushing a more radical idea (nationalize the labs) that now has an odd echo in reports that OpenAI itself is discussing a government stake. Bottom line for Umesh: this is exactly the moment to occupy Ghodsi's lane rather than Huang's or Amodei's. Uniphore doesn't need to pick a side between two of its own ecosystem partners (Nvidia and Anthropic) in a fight over existential risk — it can instead lead with concrete, operational, already-mitigable risk (data security, agent misuse, compliance) as the frame enterprise buyers can actually act on today. That also travels well alongside the NHS-Palantir and Verint/Calabrio stories below: buyers are increasingly scrutinizing AI vendor relationships on operational and governance grounds, not abstract ones — exactly the register Uniphore should keep speaking in.
